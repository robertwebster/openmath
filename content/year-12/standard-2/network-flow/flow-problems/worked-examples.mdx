# Flow problems — worked examples

## Example 1: Calculating a cut capacity

A directed network has source S, sink T, and intermediate vertices A and B:
- S→A (capacity 8), S→B (capacity 6)
- A→T (capacity 5), B→T (capacity 7), A→B (capacity 4)

Find the capacity of the cut with S-side = {S, A} and T-side = {B, T}.

<WorkedExample>
  <Step>Identify every edge that crosses from the S-side to the T-side. An edge counts only if it **starts** on the S-side and **ends** on the T-side.
  S-side = {S, A}, T-side = {B, T}.</Step>
  <Step>Check each edge:
  - S→A: both S-side — does not cross
  - S→B: S is S-side, B is T-side → **crosses** ✓ (capacity 6)
  - A→T: A is S-side, T is T-side → **crosses** ✓ (capacity 5)
  - A→B: A is S-side, B is T-side → **crosses** ✓ (capacity 4)
  - B→T: both T-side — does not cross</Step>
  <Step>Cut capacity = $6 + 5 + 4 = 15$.</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Example 2: Finding the maximum flow using the minimum-cut theorem

For the same network (S→A cap 8, S→B cap 6, A→T cap 5, B→T cap 7, A→B cap 4), find the maximum flow from S to T by evaluating all possible cuts.

<WorkedExample>
  <Step>List the meaningful cuts (there are 4 non-trivial ways to split {S, A, B, T} into two groups with S and T on opposite sides):

  **Cut 1:** S-side = {S}, T-side = {A, B, T}
  Edges crossing: S→A (8) + S→B (6) = **14**

  **Cut 2:** S-side = {S, A}, T-side = {B, T}
  Edges crossing: S→B (6) + A→T (5) + A→B (4) = **15**

  **Cut 3:** S-side = {S, B}, T-side = {A, T}
  Edges crossing: S→A (8) + B→T (7) = **15**
  (A→B goes from T-side to S-side — backward edge, not counted)

  **Cut 4:** S-side = {S, A, B}, T-side = {T}
  Edges crossing: A→T (5) + B→T (7) = **12**</Step>
  <Step>The cut capacities are: 14, 15, 15, 12. The **minimum** is **12** (Cut 4).</Step>
  <Step>By the **maximum-flow minimum-cut theorem**:
  $$\text{Maximum flow} = \text{minimum cut capacity} = 12 \text{ units}$$</Step>
  <Step>To verify: the only way to get 12 units from S to T is if both A→T (5) and B→T (7) carry their full capacity simultaneously. Working backwards: A→T uses 5 (so A needs 5 incoming); B→T uses 7 (so B needs 7 incoming). S→A carries 5 and S→B carries 6 with A→B carrying 1 (so B gets 6 + 1 = 7). Check outflow from S: $5 + 6 = 11 \neq 12$. Adjust: S→A = 8, S→B = 4, A→B = 3; then B = 4 + 3 = 7 ✓, A→T = 5 ✓. Total outflow = $8 + 4 = 12$ ✓.</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Example 3: Interpreting the minimum cut in context

A water supply system has source S (reservoir), sink T (town), and two pump stations A and B:
- S→A (capacity 10), S→B (capacity 7), A→T (capacity 8), B→T (capacity 6), A→B (capacity 3)

Find the maximum flow the system can deliver to the town.

<WorkedExample>
  <Step>Evaluate the four possible cuts:
  - **{S}**: S→A (10) + S→B (7) = 17
  - **{S, A}**: S→B (7) + A→T (8) + A→B (3) = 18
  - **{S, B}**: S→A (10) + B→T (6) = 16 (A→B is backward here — not counted)
  - **{S, A, B}**: A→T (8) + B→T (6) = 14</Step>
  <Step>Minimum cut capacity = **14** (cut through {S, A, B}).</Step>
  <Step>Maximum flow = **14 units per unit time**. The bottleneck is the total capacity of the pipes leading into the town — A→T (8) and B→T (6) — regardless of how much the earlier pipes can carry.</Step>
</WorkedExample>
