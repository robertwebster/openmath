# Integration by substitution — Worked examples

Integration by substitution (the reverse chain rule) replaces a complicated integral with a simpler one by introducing a new variable $u$.

**The strategy:** choose $u$ so that its derivative $\frac{du}{dx}$ (or a scalar multiple of it) appears in the integrand. Then replace every $x$-expression with $u$, and replace $dx$ with $\frac{du}{f'(x)}$.

---

## Example 1 — Indefinite integral (power function)

**Find $\displaystyle\int 3x^2(x^3 - 1)^5\,dx$.**

**Step 1 — Choose $u$.**

The bracket $(x^3 - 1)$ is raised to a power; choose $u = x^3 - 1$.

**Step 2 — Find $du$.**

$$\frac{du}{dx} = 3x^2 \implies du = 3x^2\,dx$$

The factor $3x^2\,dx$ appears exactly in the integrand — perfect.

**Step 3 — Substitute.**

$$\int 3x^2(x^3-1)^5\,dx = \int u^5\,du$$

**Step 4 — Integrate.**

$$\int u^5\,du = \frac{u^6}{6} + C$$

**Step 5 — Back-substitute.**

$$= \frac{(x^3-1)^6}{6} + C$$

---

## Example 2 — Definite integral (changing limits)

**Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^2 \frac{x}{(x^2+4)^2}\,dx$.**

**Step 1 — Choose $u$.**

Let $u = x^2 + 4$, so $du = 2x\,dx$, i.e. $x\,dx = \dfrac{du}{2}$.

**Step 2 — Change the limits.**

| $x$ | $u = x^2 + 4$ |
|-----|---------------|
| $0$ | $4$ |
| $2$ | $8$ |

**Step 3 — Substitute and evaluate.**

$$\int_0^2 \frac{x}{(x^2+4)^2}\,dx = \int_4^8 \frac{1}{u^2}\cdot\frac{du}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\int_4^8 u^{-2}\,du$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\left[-u^{-1}\right]_4^8 = \frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{8} = \frac{1}{16}$$

> **Key point:** once you change limits from $x$-values to $u$-values, do **not** back-substitute — integrate directly using the new limits.

---

## Example 3 — Trigonometric integrand

**Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} \sin^3 x \cos x\,dx$.**

**Step 1 — Choose $u$.**

Let $u = \sin x$, so $du = \cos x\,dx$.

**Step 2 — Change the limits.**

| $x$ | $u = \sin x$ |
|-----|-------------|
| $0$ | $0$ |
| $\pi$ | $0$ |

Both limits map to $u = 0$, so the definite integral equals zero — without needing to compute anything further.

**Formal calculation to confirm:**

$$\int_0^{\pi}\sin^3 x\cos x\,dx = \int_0^0 u^3\,du = 0$$

> **Insight:** if the upper and lower limits are equal in $u$, the integral is zero. This is a useful check and a common exam trap.
