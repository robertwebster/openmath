# Arithmetic and Cartesian form — Worked examples

Complex numbers extend the real number system by introducing $i$, defined by $i^2 = -1$. Every complex number has the form $z = a+bi$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$. The number $a$ is the real part and $b$ is the imaginary part.

---

## Key definitions

| Concept | Definition | Example with $z = 3-4i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Real part | $\text{Re}(z) = a$ | $\text{Re}(3-4i) = 3$ |
| Imaginary part | $\text{Im}(z) = b$ | $\text{Im}(3-4i) = -4$ |
| Conjugate | $\bar{z} = a-bi$ | $\overline{3-4i} = 3+4i$ |
| Modulus | $|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ | $|3-4i| = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$ |

The Argand diagram plots $z = a+bi$ as the point $(a, b)$: the real part on the horizontal axis, the imaginary part on the vertical axis.

---

## Example 1 — Multiplying complex numbers

**Find $(2+3i)(1-5i)$, expressing the answer in the form $a+bi$.**

Expand using FOIL:
$$(2+3i)(1-5i) = 2(1) + 2(-5i) + 3i(1) + 3i(-5i)$$
$$= 2 - 10i + 3i - 15i^2$$

Substitute $i^2 = -1$:
$$= 2 - 7i - 15(-1) = 2 - 7i + 15 = 17 - 7i$$

---

## Example 2 — Dividing complex numbers

**Express $\dfrac{2+i}{3-2i}$ in the form $a+bi$.**

Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, $\overline{3-2i} = 3+2i$:
$$\frac{2+i}{3-2i} = \frac{(2+i)(3+2i)}{(3-2i)(3+2i)}$$

**Denominator:** $(3-2i)(3+2i) = 3^2+2^2 = 13$ (a real number — this always works)

**Numerator:** $(2+i)(3+2i) = 6+4i+3i+2i^2 = 6+7i-2 = 4+7i$

$$\frac{2+i}{3-2i} = \frac{4+7i}{13} = \frac{4}{13} + \frac{7}{13}i$$

> **Why it works:** Multiplying $z$ by its conjugate $\bar{z}$ always gives a real number: $z\bar{z} = a^2+b^2 = |z|^2$.

---

## Example 3 — Finding a complex square root

**Find all $z \in \mathbb{C}$ satisfying $z^2 = 3+4i$.**

Let $z = a+bi$. Expanding:
$$z^2 = (a+bi)^2 = a^2 - b^2 + 2abi$$

Equate real and imaginary parts with $3+4i$:
$$a^2 - b^2 = 3 \quad \cdots (1)$$
$$2ab = 4 \implies ab = 2 \quad \cdots (2)$$

From (2): $b = \dfrac{2}{a}$. Substitute into (1):
$$a^2 - \frac{4}{a^2} = 3$$
$$a^4 - 3a^2 - 4 = 0$$
$$(a^2-4)(a^2+1) = 0$$

Since $a \in \mathbb{R}$: $a^2 = 4$, so $a = \pm 2$.

- $a = 2 \Rightarrow b = 1$: $z = 2+i$
- $a = -2 \Rightarrow b = -1$: $z = -2-i$

**Check:** $(2+i)^2 = 4+4i+i^2 = 4+4i-1 = 3+4i$ ✓

The two square roots of $3+4i$ are $2+i$ and $-2-i$ (which is $-(2+i)$, as expected).
