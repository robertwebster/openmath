# The definite integral

## Example 1 — Evaluating a definite integral

**Problem:** Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^3 (x^2 - 2x + 4)\,dx$.

**Step 1 — Find the antiderivative.**

$$F(x) = \frac{x^3}{3} - x^2 + 4x$$

**Step 2 — Apply the limits using the notation $\Big[F(x)\Big]_1^3$.**

$$\left[\frac{x^3}{3} - x^2 + 4x\right]_1^3 = F(3) - F(1)$$

**Step 3 — Calculate each value.**

$$F(3) = \frac{27}{3} - 9 + 12 = 9 - 9 + 12 = 12$$

$$F(1) = \frac{1}{3} - 1 + 4 = \frac{1}{3} + 3 = \frac{10}{3}$$

**Step 4 — Subtract.**

$$\int_1^3 (x^2 - 2x + 4)\,dx = 12 - \frac{10}{3} = \frac{36 - 10}{3} = \frac{26}{3}$$

## Example 2 — Signed area and regions below the axis

**Problem:** Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\,dx$ and explain the result geometrically.

**Step 1 — Find the antiderivative and apply the limits.**

$$\int_0^{2\pi} \sin x\,dx = \Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^{2\pi} = -\cos(2\pi) - (-\cos 0) = -1 + 1 = 0$$

**Step 2 — Interpret the result.**

The definite integral is zero, but the curve is not zero everywhere. On $[0, \pi]$, $\sin x \geq 0$ (area above the axis), and on $[\pi, 2\pi]$, $\sin x \leq 0$ (area below the axis). The two areas are equal in magnitude, so they cancel:

$$\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx = 2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx = -2$$

**Key point:** The definite integral gives *signed* area. To find the *total enclosed area*, integrate on each sub-interval separately and add the absolute values.

## Example 3 — Applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

**Problem:** Define $G(x) = \displaystyle\int_2^x \frac{1}{t^2 + 1}\,dt$. Find $G'(x)$ and hence find $G'(2)$.

**Step 1 — Apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.**

The theorem states: if $G(x) = \displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$, then $G'(x) = f(x)$.

Therefore:
$$G'(x) = \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}$$

**Step 2 — Evaluate at $x = 2$.**

$$G'(2) = \frac{1}{2^2 + 1} = \frac{1}{5}$$

Note that the lower limit $a = 2$ plays no role in the derivative — only the integrand evaluated at the upper limit matters.
